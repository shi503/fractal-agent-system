# Notification fatigue — product principles

Five principles distilled from the user study. These are meant to settle arguments without reopening the research every time.

## P1 — Never tell someone what they just did

A notification triggered by the reader's own action is pure noise and it costs trust disproportionately. Every participant raised this unprompted. It is not a preference; it is suppressed unconditionally.

## P2 — Only two things are allowed to interrupt

Assignment and direct reply. Everything else waits for the roll-up. The bar for adding a third interrupt category is a new study, not an opinion.

## P3 — The roll-up is the product, the interrupt is the exception

Design the digest first and treat interrupts as the carve-out. This inverts the usual instinct, where the per-event notification is built first and a digest is bolted on later as a volume remedy. If the digest is an afterthought it reads like one, and then nobody uses the surface the study says they wanted.

## P4 — Ship quiet

A team that gets shouted at on day one uninstalls before it finds the settings screen. A team that hears too little asks for more. Those two failure modes are not symmetric, and the asymmetry is the whole argument for the starting posture.

## P5 — Volume is not tuned, it is toggled

The study's central behavioural finding: users do not gradually adjust. They flip between everything-on and everything-off, and the flip is triggered by a single bad experience in either direction. Consequences: a granular settings screen will not save a loud product, and one badly-scoped interrupt category can undo a well-tuned one.

## What these principles do not cover

Whether quiet hours suppress or defer is still open. Per-board overrides are backlog. Nothing here says anything about the delivery channel — email versus in-app is an orthogonal question and should not be argued using this page.
